Discussion question
To what extent is digital currency the future of society?
Source Document

The Guardian view on the digital pound: an impetuous idea with a risky momentum of its own

Two years ago, the House of Lords looked into whether the Bank of England should issue digital pounds to be held in electronic wallets. Peers were unconvinced, asking if this wasn't “a solution in search of a problem”. Last December, MPs on the Treasury select committee said the same thing. The government's response, however, was to tell the public to prepare for a “Britcoin” to be in use by the end of the decade.

Physical money has been around for thousands of years for good reason. Cash is convenient, reliable and anonymous. It seems unlikely that these advantages could be replicated online. State-backed e-cash might look inevitable to officials who say money is already digital, pointing to cryptocurrencies and smartphone payments. But central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) are different. A Britcoin would shift the management burden from commercial banks to Threadneedle Street.

Paul Tucker, a former deputy Bank governor, argued in his book Global Discord that the West is responding to China's digital renminbi, which potentially gives the state social control through direct access to banking transactions. Yet the UK government can already spy on commercial bank accounts. It doesn't do so without good reason. Brett Scott, a former broker and the author of Cloudmoney, accepts that there is a geopolitical rivalry. The difference, he says, is that private corporations can push their agendas harder in the West than in Beijing.

During the pandemic, finance and technology firms accelerated moves towards a cashless society, which saw hitherto unimaginable levels of surveillance and data extraction. A world without notes and coins would hurt those least able to afford it. Poor and elderly people often prefer to budget with cash. A million UK adults don't have a bank account. The Royal Society of Arts calculated that going cash-free risked 10 million Britons losing control of their finances. The prospect of a cashless society also unnerved central banks. They worried about being dominated by commercial banks. Their answer: central banks should have their own digital currencies.

However, if central banks pushed CBDCs, they risked destabilising the financial institutions they oversaw. Central banks can't go bankrupt. People would vote with their feet if they could effectively open accounts at the Bank of England. According to a 2019 survey of 23 central banks, 82% of respondents worried that digital currencies could cause runs on banks. To address concerns, caps on CBDC holdings have been suggested. But limits may put off many from using digital pounds.

Mr Scott rightly compares the digitalisation of money to Uberisation, a process where convenience is traded for societal threats that older behaviours kept in check. That's why we need a debate rather than passively accepting state-backed digital money. Facebook's global cryptocurrency, Diem, was killed off by money-laundering concerns. But pervasive monitoring of spending changes society's psychology for the worse.

Right-wing conspiracy theories have been launched by excitable talk about the potential to place conditions on who can use digital money, for what, and with whom. Technically feasible? Eventually. Politically realisable? Gradually. Desirable? Well, that depends on your perspective. Maybe in developing countries, a digital central bank currency could be used, as was attempted briefly in Ecuador, to stymie an overly powerful, and US-aligned, domestic finance sector. But for the UK, it is an idea whose time has yet to come.

05 Build a Strong Argument

Un argument oral efficace suit quatre temps : IDEA → EXPLAIN → EXAMPLE → LINK.

Why this matters
Idea only, too weakDigital currency is convenient.
+ Explain, betterDigital currency is convenient because it allows instant payments without needing physical cash or a bank branch.
+ Example, stronger…For example, mobile-money systems such as M-Pesa already let people in Kenya pay and transfer money using only a basic phone.
Part 1, Advantages Build one argument

Modèle, Part 1

Idea , Digital currency could make everyday payments faster and more efficient.

Explain , Because it removes the need for physical handling of cash and lets transactions happen instantly, even across borders.

Example , For example, mobile-money systems such as M-Pesa already let millions of people in Kenya pay and transfer money using only a basic phone.

Link , This shows that digital currency can genuinely modernise how society exchanges money, at least where infrastructure allows it.

Part 2, Limits Build one argument

Modèle, Part 2

Idea , Digital currency could increase surveillance of ordinary spending.

Explain , Because every digital transaction can be recorded and monitored by banks, companies or governments, unlike anonymous cash.

Example , For example, the text notes that programmable money could let authorities decide who can spend money, where and for what purpose.

Link , This shows that, if left unregulated, digital currency could weaken individual privacy and freedom.

08 Nuance Your Answer

« To what extent » n'accepte jamais une réponse tout-ou-rien. Utilisez may, can, could, to some extent, depending on, unless…

Too binaryDigital currency will replace cash.
NuancedDigital currency may become increasingly important, but whether it can replace cash will depend on public trust, accessibility and regulation.
Rewrite this claim with more nuance:« Programmable digital money is dangerous. » Utilisez au moins un mot de nuance.

Modèle, nuance

Programmable digital money can raise concerns about control, but this risk largely depends on the legal safeguards put in place, and would not necessarily apply unless governments chose to restrict its use.

Introduction

Digital currency is likely to play an important role in the future, but I do not think it will completely replace cash. It offers major benefits, but it also creates significant economic and social risks. I will first consider its main advantages before examining its limits.

Part 1. Advantages
  • Citizens already use contactless payments, online banking and smartphone apps.Source : connaissances
  • A CBDC could help central banks remain powerful as private firms increasingly control payments.Source : texte, §4
  • It could make payments faster and cheaper, especially for international transfers.Source : connaissances
  • It supports innovation, this echoes Schumpeter's idea of "creative destruction".Source : connaissances
  • In developing countries it could improve financial inclusion where banks are weak.Source : connaissances
Part 2. Limits
  • Cash remains convenient, reliable and anonymous, digital money may not offer the same privacy.Source : texte, §2
  • A cashless society could exclude poor, elderly and unbanked people.Source : texte, §4
  • Spending can be monitored by governments or private companies.Source : texte, §3 & §6
  • Programmable money raises fears of social control, who can pay, where, for what.Source : texte, §7
  • As Stiglitz argues, technological progress can deepen exclusion without protective institutions.Source : connaissances
Part 3. Overall assessment
  • Digital currency should coexist with cash, citizens need both.Source : connaissances
  • A public debate is necessary before adopting state-backed digital money.Source : texte, §6
  • Strong legal rules should protect against surveillance and data misuse.Source : connaissances
  • Caps on CBDC holdings could reduce bank-run risk, but may discourage use.Source : texte, §5
  • As Keynes showed, confidence is central, digital money must build trust, not fear.Source : connaissances
Conclusion

Overall, digital currency is likely to become an important part of society, because economic life is already highly digital. But it is not the whole future: cash still protects privacy and accessibility. The best outcome is a balanced model where digital money coexists with cash, controlled by strong democratic rules. Its success will largely depend on regulation, accessibility and public trust.
