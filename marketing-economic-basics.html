1

The marketing mix: the 4 Ps

Marketing is everything a company does to understand its customers and sell to them. The marketing mix sums it up in four decisions, the 4 Ps: product, price, place (where and how it is sold) and promotion (how it is communicated). The four must be consistent.

You could say:
“The four elements of the marketing mix must be consistent: product, price, place and promotion must all send the same message.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

2

Segmentation, targeting and positioning

Customers do not all want the same thing. Segmentation divides the market into groups with similar needs, for example by age or by lifestyle. Targeting means choosing which groups to serve. Positioning is the image the brand wants to have in the mind of these customers, compared with its competitors.

You could say:
“A brand cannot please everybody. It must choose its target segments and position itself clearly against its competitors.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

3

Price and perceived value

Customers do not only compare prices; they compare what they pay with what they get. This is perceived value: quality, image, service, emotion. With digital tools, some companies use dynamic pricing: the price changes with demand, as for plane tickets. It can increase revenue, but customers may find it unfair.

You could say:
“Customers buy value, not just a price. Dynamic pricing can increase revenue, but it can also look unfair and damage trust.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

4

Winning and keeping customers

Winning a new customer usually costs more than keeping an existing one: advertising, promotions, discounts. A loyal customer buys again and again, and often recommends the brand. Customer loyalty is therefore a source of stable revenue. But a brand also needs new customers, for example younger ones, to survive.

You could say:
“Building loyalty is a long-term investment: keeping a customer usually costs less than winning a new one.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

5

Brand identity and rebranding

A brand is more than a name: it is a logo, a style, values and emotions. Loyal customers often feel that the brand belongs to them. This is why rebranding, changing the image of a brand, is risky. A sudden change can look like a betrayal; a gradual change, tested and explained, is safer.

You could say:
“A brand must change with its customers, not against them. A sudden rebranding can destroy years of loyalty.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

6

Social media and influencers

Traditional advertising is a one-way message. Social media create a two-way conversation: the brand speaks, and customers answer, share and comment, in public. Even small companies can reach a large audience at low cost. Influencers are popular content creators paid by brands; they often seem more authentic than an advert.

You could say:
“Social media are double-edged: they help brands to reach customers cheaply, but customers can also use them to criticise the brand in public.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

7

E-reputation and crisis communication

E-reputation is the image of a brand on the internet: reviews, comments, posts. A backlash, a wave of public anger, can spread in a few hours. Crisis communication is the way a company answers: listen, answer quickly, explain its choices and, when needed, apologise.

You could say:
“On social media, a crisis can spread in hours. The brand must listen, answer quickly and explain its choices.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

8

Personalisation, data and AI

Instead of sending the same message to everybody, brands now use customer data to personalise their offers, recommendations and sometimes prices. Generative AI can create advertisements and campaigns in minutes. Well used, personalisation helps customers; badly used, it feels intrusive.

You could say:
“Data and AI allow brands to personalise their offers. This can help customers, but only if they trust the way their data is used.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

9

Consent, ethics and trust

Personalisation depends on personal data, so customers must give their consent, as the European GDPR requires. Trust also depends on authenticity: fake reviews, hidden advertising or invented influencers mislead people. A brand that deceives may gain sales today, but it loses trust, and trust is slow to rebuild.

You could say:
“A brand that misuses data or misleads its customers may win in the short term, but it loses their trust in the long term.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.
