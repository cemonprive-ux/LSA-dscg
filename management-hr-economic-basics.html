1

Attracting and keeping talent

Companies compete to hire skilled people: this is the war for talent. When an employee leaves, the company loses money and time, because it must recruit and train someone new. Staff turnover is the share of employees who leave each year. Keeping good employees, or retention, is therefore a key goal.

You could say:
“In the war for talent, keeping good employees is essential. Every departure costs the company money and time.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

2

Office, remote and hybrid work

Since the pandemic, remote work has become normal. In hybrid work, employees share their week between the office and home. Flexibility helps to attract talent, but some large firms are now asking staff to come back to the office more often, because they see real benefits in working face to face.

You could say:
“Hybrid work gives employees flexibility, but managers must set clear rules to keep the team together.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

3

Productivity and performance

Productivity compares what a person produces with the time spent. Being present is not the same as being productive. Modern management judges performance mainly on results, not on hours at the desk. This needs clear objectives, known by everybody.

You could say:
“Performance should be judged on results, not on hours of presence. This requires clear objectives.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

4

Wellbeing and burnout

Wellbeing at work is now a management issue. Too much work for too long can lead to burnout: deep physical and mental exhaustion. Digital tools also blur the line between work and private life, which makes work-life balance harder. Tired employees are less productive and more likely to leave.

You could say:
“Wellbeing is not a luxury. Exhausted employees are less productive and more likely to leave.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

5

Trust and control: employee monitoring

Digital tools allow employee monitoring: badge swipes, connections, dashboards. Managers say these tools help to organise teams and to treat everyone the same way. Employees often feel watched: trust falls and motivation suffers. Monitoring is more acceptable when it is transparent, proportionate and explained.

You could say:
“Monitoring is not wrong in itself, but it must be transparent and proportionate. Otherwise, it destroys trust.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

6

From hierarchy to agility

In a traditional hierarchy, decisions go down from the top, level by level. In an agile organisation, small teams decide quickly and work directly with the data. The manager becomes less a controller and more a facilitator: someone who explains, supports and removes obstacles.

You could say:
“Management models are becoming more agile. The manager is less a controller and more a facilitator of change.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

7

AI in human resources

Human resources (HR) manage the people of a company: recruitment, pay, training, careers. AI can now sort job applications, find skills gaps and predict who might leave. It saves time, but it can be biased, because it learns from past decisions. Clear rules and a human decision remain necessary.

You could say:
“AI can help HR departments to save time, but it can reproduce past bias. The final decision must stay human.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

8

Human capital and training

Human capital is the knowledge and skills of the employees. Like machines, it is a resource that companies must invest in. With AI, skills become out of date quickly, so a workforce that is not trained is a business risk. Training must be short, continuous and open to all. It also reduces the fear of change.

You could say:
“Training is an investment in human capital. In a fast-changing world, a company that does not train its staff takes a real risk.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

9

Corporate and national cultures

Corporate culture is the set of values and habits shared inside a company: “the way we do things here”. Teams are also more and more international, so managers work with people from different national cultures, who may communicate differently and see authority differently. Cultural awareness prevents misunderstandings.

You could say:
“In international teams, cultural awareness is a key skill: it prevents misunderstandings and builds trust.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.
