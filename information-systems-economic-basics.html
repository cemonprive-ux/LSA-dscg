1

Data and information systems

An information system is everything a company uses to collect, store and process information: software, computers, networks, and the people who use them. Data is the raw material: figures, names, transactions. Once it is organised and analysed, data becomes information that helps managers to decide.

You could say:
“Data is now a key resource for companies. A good information system turns raw data into information that helps managers to decide.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

2

Automation and productivity

Productivity is how much is produced in one hour of work. Automation means that a machine or a program does a task instead of a person, usually a repetitive one. When technology increases productivity, a company can produce more in the same time, or the same in less time.

You could say:
“Technology increases productivity: it takes over repetitive tasks, so employees can produce more in less time.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

3

Generative AI

Generative AI is a program trained on huge amounts of text and images. It can write, summarise, translate and code in a few seconds. But it does not understand the world as a human does: it predicts a probable answer. It is a fast assistant, not an expert who is always right.

You could say:
“Generative AI works like a tireless assistant: it is fast and useful, but its work must always be checked by a human.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

4

Jobs and skills

When machines can do a task, some jobs disappear, many jobs change and new jobs appear. The main effect is a change in the skills that companies need. Upskilling means improving your skills in your present job; reskilling means learning new skills for a different job.

You could say:
“AI will probably not end work, but it will change the skills that companies need. That is why upskilling and reskilling are essential.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

5

Sharing the gains

When technology saves time, somebody receives the benefit. These productivity gains can go to shareholders as higher profit, to employees as higher wages or more free time, or to customers as lower prices. The result depends on human choices, not on the technology itself.

You could say:
“The real question is who benefits from the productivity gains. If they are shared fairly, employees stay motivated.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

6

The risks of AI: bias and hallucination

AI makes two typical mistakes. Bias: the program learns from old data, so it can repeat unfair patterns hidden in this data. Hallucination: the program gives an answer that sounds confident but is false. In both cases, the danger is to trust the machine without checking.

You could say:
“AI can be biased, and it can also produce false answers with great confidence. So human supervision remains necessary.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

7

Data protection and cybersecurity

Personal data is any information about a person: a name, an address, a salary. Companies must protect it, and employees should never put confidential data into a public AI tool. Cybersecurity means protecting systems against attacks. AI also helps criminals: it makes scams, such as false emails, more convincing.

You could say:
“Powerful tools need strong safeguards. Companies must protect personal data and train their staff against cyberattacks.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

8

Regulation and human oversight

In the European Union, two laws frame technology. The GDPR protects personal data. The AI Act sets rules according to the level of risk: the more dangerous the use, the stricter the rules. Both ask for a human in the loop: a person who checks the system and stays responsible for the decision.

You could say:
“The debate has moved from what AI can do to how it should be controlled. With the AI Act, the stricter rules apply to the riskier uses.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.
