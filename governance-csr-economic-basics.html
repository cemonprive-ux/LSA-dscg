1

Corporate governance

Corporate governance is the way a company is directed and controlled. The shareholders own the company. They elect a board of directors, which supervises the managers who run the business every day. Good governance means clear rules, transparency and control of those who hold power.

You could say:
“Good governance means that managers are controlled by the board, and that the company is transparent about its decisions.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

2

Shareholders and stakeholders

A shareholder owns shares in the company and expects a profit. A stakeholder is anyone affected by the company: employees, customers, suppliers, local communities, the state. For some people, a company exists only to make profit for its shareholders. For others, it must answer to all its stakeholders.

You could say:
“There are two views of the company. For some, it only has to make profit for its shareholders; for others, it is responsible to all its stakeholders.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

3

CSR and ESG

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is what a company decides to do for society and the environment, beyond what the law requires. ESG (environmental, social and governance) is the set of criteria that investors use to measure this behaviour. CSR is the commitment; ESG is the measurement.

You could say:
“CSR is the commitment of the company; ESG criteria are the tools that investors use to measure it.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

4

Externalities and the polluter pays principle

An externality is a cost that a company creates but does not pay. Pollution is the classic example: the factory pollutes, and society pays for the damage. With a tax or a carbon price, the company must pay for its emissions: this is the polluter pays principle.

You could say:
“Pollution is an externality: the company creates the cost, but society pays for it. A carbon price forces the polluter to pay.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

5

The business case: does responsibility pay?

Critics say that companies exist to make profit, and that social projects cost money without a clear return. Supporters answer that a responsible firm has a better reputation, more loyal customers and more motivated employees, and avoids expensive scandals. Responsibility is a form of long-term risk management.

You could say:
“Being responsible has a cost in the short term, but it protects the company in the long term. It is a form of risk management.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

6

Greenwashing

Greenwashing means presenting a company or a product as greener than it really is. Typical examples are vague claims such as “eco-friendly” or “carbon neutral” without any evidence. Stakeholders now ask for proof, and misleading claims can lead to complaints or fines. A commitment must be measurable and verifiable.

You could say:
“A green claim without evidence is greenwashing. To be credible, a commitment must be measurable and verifiable.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

7

From voluntary to compulsory

For a long time, CSR was voluntary. It is now becoming compulsory. In the European Union, the CSRD obliges the largest companies to publish their environmental and social impact, and duty-of-vigilance laws make firms responsible for what their suppliers do abroad. In some markets, however, there is a backlash against ESG rules.

You could say:
“Responsibility is moving from a voluntary promise to a legal duty, even if there is a backlash against these rules in some markets.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

8

Going green in a globalised economy

Rules stop at borders, but companies and pollution do not. A firm in a country with strict environmental rules has higher costs than a competitor in a country with weak rules, and it may be tempted to move its production there. This is why companies ask for a level playing field: the same rules for everybody.

You could say:
“In a globalised economy, strict rules in one country can simply move pollution to another. That is why firms ask for a level playing field.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.

9

Technology and responsibility

Artificial intelligence can support good governance: it helps firms to monitor their supply chains and to measure their emissions. But it raises its own problems: high energy use, bias, and threats to privacy and jobs. A company cannot call itself responsible if it ignores the effects of its own technology.

You could say:
“AI can help companies to measure their impact, but it also uses a lot of energy and can be biased. A responsible company must control its own technology.”

Say it in your own words first, then check.
